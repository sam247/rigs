# Planning Kitchen Electrics During a Renovation

Getting the electrics right is one of the most critical – and often most overlooked – parts of a kitchen renovation. Unlike other rooms where you can add a socket or run a new cable later, a finished kitchen leaves almost no room for changes without tearing out cabinets, worktops, or floor coverings. The electrical work needs to be planned from the start and sequenced carefully with the kitchen fitter, plumber, and gas engineer.

This article explains what you need to know as a homeowner: the regulations that apply, the circuits your modern kitchen is likely to need, practical decisions about socket and lighting placement, and how to coordinate the work so nothing gets missed.

## The First Step: Electrical Planning Before Cabinets Go In

Electrical planning should begin before you finalise your kitchen layout or order any cabinets. The reason is simple: the position of every socket, switch, and appliance connection point depends on where your cabinets, worktops, and island will sit.

Start by listing every appliance you plan to use – now and in the foreseeable future. This includes:

- Oven and hob (and whether they are gas, electric, or induction)
- Fridge and freezer (or a combined fridge-freezer)
- Washing machine and dishwasher
- Microwave (integrated or freestanding)
- Extractor hood
- Under-cabinet lighting
- Kettle, coffee machine, toaster, and other countertop appliances
- Any future additions such as a wine cooler or boiling-water tap

From this list, you and your electrician can work out the total electrical load and decide how many new circuits are needed. Many older homes have a consumer unit (fuse box) that cannot handle the demands of a modern kitchen. If yours needs an upgrade, it is far better to discover that before the renovation starts.

## Understanding Part P and Building Regulations

In the UK, kitchen electrical work is covered by Part P of the Building Regulations. This applies whenever you add a new circuit, replace a consumer unit, or carry out any electrical work in a kitchen – defined for Part P purposes as a room with a sink. Work within 3 metres of a sink is also notifiable.

Notifiable work must be carried out by a registered electrician who is a member of a Competent Person Scheme (such as NICEIC, NAPIT, or ELECSA). They can self-certify the work and provide a Building Regulations compliance certificate. If you use an electrician who is not registered, you will need to notify your local building control department in advance and pay for an inspection.

**What is notifiable?**
- Installing a new circuit (for example, a new ring final for sockets or a dedicated cooker circuit)
- Replacing or upgrading the consumer unit
- Adding a new circuit from an existing consumer unit
- Any work in a kitchen that involves a new fixed electrical installation

**What is non-notifiable?**
- Replacing like-for-like socket fronts or switches (as long as the circuit is not extended)
- Repairing or maintaining existing installations
- Adding a spur from an existing ring circuit (though this is best checked with your electrician)

Keep the certificate from any notifiable work. You will need it when you sell your home – missing certificates can delay a sale and cost thousands to rectify.

## Dedicated Circuits: What Your Kitchen Actually Needs

A modern kitchen typically needs more than one circuit. Running everything off a single ring final for sockets is no longer safe or practical. Dedicated circuits prevent overloading, allow you to isolate individual appliances for maintenance, and meet the requirements of BS 7671 (the IET Wiring Regulations).

Here are the circuits most kitchens require:

**Cooker circuit (32A or 45A)**
A dedicated radial circuit for the cooker. If you have a separate induction hob and oven, each may need its own circuit. Induction hobs draw significant current and typically require a 32A supply on 6mm² cable. A combined cooker and hob can be supplied by a 45A circuit on 10mm² cable.

**Socket ring circuit (32A)**
The main ring final for kitchen worktop sockets. This should be separate from the downstairs ring to avoid tripping the whole floor if an appliance faults. Many modern kitchens have **two** dedicated 32A ring circuits for sockets to spread the load.

**Dedicated radial circuits for fixed appliances (16A or 20A)**
Appliances that are built in or fixed in position – washing machine, dishwasher, fridge-freezer, microwave, tumble dryer – should each have their own fused spur or a dedicated 20A radial circuit. This makes it easy to isolate an appliance for servicing without affecting other sockets.

**Extractor hood (fused spur)**
A switched fused spur above the worktop for the extractor, typically 3A or 5A fuse.

**Under-cabinet lighting (via a 5A or 6A lighting circuit)**
Usually served from the kitchen lighting circuit or a separate lighting circuit with its own switch. Under-cabinet lights need their own drivers and should be switched independently from the ceiling lights.

**Ceiling lighting (6A circuit)**
A standard lighting circuit, often with multiple switches for different zones (overhead, island pendants, task lighting).

**Additional circuits for islands**
If you have a kitchen island, you will need socket outlets and possibly lighting. Options include pop-up sockets mounted in the worktop or floor-mounted power units. These must be positioned to avoid water splashes – Regulation 553.1.3 requires that socket outlets are not easily accessible to someone using a fixed appliance.

## Socket Placement and Lighting: Practical Layout Decisions

Getting sockets in the right places makes the kitchen usable and safe. Here are the practical rules of thumb, based on current regulations and best practice.

**Worktop sockets**
Aim for at least 6–8 double sockets along the worktop, spaced roughly every 1–1.5 metres. They should be mounted at least 150mm above the worktop to comply with Part M (accessibility) and to avoid splashback from the sink. Do not place sockets directly behind a sink or hob – the minimum distance from a sink is 300mm horizontally, and from a hob 100mm (but more is safer).

**Sockets behind appliances**
Every integrated appliance – dishwasher, washing machine, fridge-freezer – needs an accessible electrical connection. This is usually a switched fused spur located either above the appliance behind a cabinet panel or next to it in an adjacent cabinet. Do not hide sockets where they cannot be reached without pulling out the appliance.

**Island sockets**
If your island includes a sink or a hob, sockets must be placed where they cannot be splashed. Pop-up sockets are a common solution. Alternatively, consider floor-mounted power units at the base of the island. Remember that island sockets count as worktop sockets, so they should be on a separate ring or radial circuit.

**Lighting zones**
Think about how you use the different parts of the kitchen. Good lighting design includes:
- General ceiling lighting (recessed downlights or a pendant grid)
- Task lighting over the main work surfaces (under-cabinet LED strips)
- Island lighting (pendants or track lighting)
- Ambient lighting (dimmer zones for evenings)
Each zone should have its own switch or dimmer, and it is worth running a separate lighting circuit for the kitchen so a single tripped breaker does not leave you in the dark.

## Coordination With Other Trades and the Installation Sequence

A kitchen renovation involves multiple trades working in sequence. The electrical work is not a single job – it is typically six or seven separate visits done in the right order. Here is a typical sequence:

1. **First fix** – After the room is stripped back to brick/plaster, the electrician runs all new cables, installs back boxes, and drills holes for lighting. This happens before any plastering.
2. **Plumbing first fix** – The plumber runs water and waste pipes behind the walls.
3. **Gas first fix** – If you have a gas hob or boiler, the gas engineer runs the pipework.
4. **Plastering** – The walls are plastered, covering the cables and pipes.
5. **Second fix** – After plastering, the electrician returns to connect sockets, switches, lighting, and appliance points. This happens after the kitchen units are fitted but before the worktops go on.
6. **Final connections** – Once the units and appliances are installed, the electrician connects the cooker, hob, extractor, and under-cabinet lights, and tests the whole installation.

**Why this matters:** If you miss a cable run during the first fix, it’s very difficult to add later. If the electrician does not know exactly where each appliance will sit, sockets can end up behind cupboards or too far from the appliance.

**Coordinating with the kitchen fitter**
Share your electrical plan with the kitchen fitter as soon as the layout is finalised. Mark the positions of cabinets, worktops, and appliances on a floor plan, then give that to the electrician. The fitter needs to know where sockets are so they can cut openings in carcasses. The electrician needs to know exactly where the cooker, hob, and extractor will be so the cables reach the right positions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need planning permission for kitchen electrical work?**
No, planning permission is not required for internal electrical work in a kitchen. However, you must comply with Part P of the Building Regulations. If the work is notifiable (new circuits, consumer unit change, or work within 3m of a sink), you need a registered electrician who can self-certify.

**Can I do the electrical work myself?**
You can do non-notifiable work yourself (e.g. replacing a socket), but notifiable work must be carried out by a registered electrician. Even for small jobs, working on a kitchen circuit without the right knowledge is risky – water and electricity are close together, and a mistake could cause a fire or fatal shock. It is wise to use a professional for any new circuits or alterations.

**How much does kitchen electrical work cost?**
Costs vary widely depending on the size of the kitchen, the number of new circuits, and whether the consumer unit needs upgrading. Expect the electrical work to be a significant part of your overall kitchen budget, because you are paying for multiple dedicated circuits, compliance with current regulations, and the labour of coordinating with other trades. The best way to get an accurate figure is to ask a registered electrician to quote after they have seen your plans.

**Do I need a separate circuit for a dishwasher?**
Yes. Dishwashers, washing machines, and other fixed appliances should be on a dedicated radial circuit (typically 16A or 20A) or a fused spur taken from the kitchen ring. This prevents the appliance from overloading the socket ring and allows you to isolate it for servicing.

**Can I hide sockets in a kitchen island?**
Yes, but they must be accessible and positioned to avoid water risk. Pop-up sockets that sit flush in the worktop are a popular choice. Alternatively, you can use floor-mounted power units at the base of the island. Always check with your electrician that the installation complies with BS 7671 Regulation 553.1.3.

**What happens if I sell my house and don’t have a Part P certificate?**
The buyer’s solicitor will typically ask for certificates for any notifiable electrical work carried out in the last few years. If you cannot provide them, you may need to pay for an Electrical Installation Condition Report (EICR) to prove the work is safe. This can cost from a few hundred pounds into the thousands depending on the size of the installation.

## What To Do Next

The most important step is to involve a qualified electrician early. Do not wait until the kitchen units arrive. Share your appliance list and a floor plan showing where everything will go, and ask for a detailed quote that itemises each circuit, any consumer unit upgrades, and the cost of certification.
