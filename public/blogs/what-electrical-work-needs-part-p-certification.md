# What Electrical Work Needs Part P Certification?

In England and Wales, most fixed electrical work in homes must comply with Part P of the Building Regulations. **Notifiable work** — which must be certified and notified to building control — includes new circuits, consumer unit replacements, and electrical work in kitchens, bathrooms, and gardens. Some minor alterations to existing circuits are non-notifiable but must still be safe and compliant with BS 7671.

Scotland uses a different building standards system; Northern Ireland has separate rules. This guide focuses on **England and Wales**, where RIGS carries out domestic work from Tring.

Sources: [Electrical Safety First — Part P](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/find-a-registered-electrician/part-p-of-the-building-regulations), [IET / Electrical Safety First guidance on notifiable work](https://electrical.theiet.org/bs-7671-18th-edition-wiring-regulations/building-regulations/part-p-england-and-wales/).

## What Part P Requires

Part P exists so domestic electrical work meets safety standards and is independently verifiable. Homeowners must be able to prove compliance — failure is a **criminal offence** under building regulations, and local authorities can require removal or alteration of non-compliant work ([Electrical Safety First](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/find-a-registered-electrician/part-p-of-the-building-regulations)).

Work must meet **BS 7671** (IET Wiring Regulations). Registered electricians in competent person schemes (NICEIC, NAPIT, etc.) can **self-certify** notifiable work and notify on your behalf.

## Notifiable Work (Requires Certification and Notification)

Typically includes:

**New circuits**  
Any new circuit from the consumer unit — additional socket ring, lighting circuit, cooker supply, shower circuit, outdoor feed, etc.

**Consumer unit replacement or major alteration**  
Replacing or adding consumer units is notifiable. See [consumer unit replacement cost planning](/blog/how-much-does-a-consumer-unit-replacement-cost).

**Work in special locations**  
Kitchens, bathrooms, shower rooms, and certain garden/outdoor locations have additional protection requirements. Most work in these areas is notifiable.

**Full or partial rewires**  
Rewiring involves new circuits and certification throughout.

**Extensions and structural alterations**  
New fixed wiring in extensions is notifiable — plan with [extension electrics guidance](/blog/electrics-for-a-house-extension-planning-guide).

**Outdoor installations**  
New garden circuits, outdoor sockets on new feeds, and exterior lighting on new circuits. See [garden electrics](/blog/garden-electrics-outdoor-sockets-lighting-garden-rooms).

**Central heating controls and fixed equipment**  
When involving new circuit work (not simple like-for-like control swaps in all cases — confirm with your electrician).

If unsure, assume notifiable and use a registered installer.

## Non-Notifiable Work (Still Must Be Safe)

Electrical Safety First and the IET describe minor work that may not require notification in England, including:

- Replacing accessories (**socket outlets, control switches, ceiling roses**) on **existing** circuits — where not in special locations and not involving new circuits
- Replacing damaged cable for a single circuit in same location in some circumstances
- Maintenance and like-for-like repairs

**Non-notifiable does not mean DIY-is-always-fine.** Work must still comply with BS 7671. Incompetent work can kill and void insurance. Many homeowners prefer a registered electrician even for minor jobs.

Adding a socket by spurring an existing circuit can fall into grey areas depending on location and design — ask a registered electrician rather than self-assessing.

## Certificates You Should Receive

| Document | When issued |
| --- | --- |
| **Electrical Installation Certificate (EIC)** | New circuits, consumer units, rewires |
| **Minor Electrical Installation Works Certificate (MEIWC)** | Minor notifiable alterations to existing circuits |
| **Electrical Installation Condition Report (EICR)** | Inspection/testing only — not a work certificate |
| **Building Regulations Compliance Certificate** | Confirms Part P notification for notifiable work |

Keep all certificates for resale, landlord compliance, and insurance. See also [electrical checks when buying a house](/blog/electrical-checks-when-buying-a-house-in-tring).

## Registered Electrician vs Building Control

**Route 1 — Registered competent person (recommended)**  
Electrician self-certifies and notifies scheme operator. You receive compliance certificate without lodging building notice yourself.

**Route 2 — Unregistered electrician + building control**  
Building notice, fees, and third-party inspection/testing required before work or in stages — slower and often costlier.

**Route 3 — DIY**  
Possible for some non-notifiable minor work only if competent. Notifiable DIY requires building control involvement — most homeowners should not attempt.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

- Difficulty selling — buyer’s solicitor requests certificates
- Local authority enforcement
- Insurance claim issues after fire or shock
- Rework costs exceeding original saving from uncertificated cheap work

## Scotland and Northern Ireland

**Scotland** uses the Building Standards system — electrical work must comply with Scottish regulations and BS 7671, but Part P as described for England and Wales does not apply in the same form. Use a registered electrician familiar with local building standards.

**Northern Ireland** has separate building control arrangements. Homeowners there should follow NI guidance and use registered installers.

## Building Control Notification Routes

If work is notifiable and you use a **non-registered installer** (not recommended), you must notify local building control before work starts and arrange inspection. Fees and delays apply.

Registered **competent person scheme** members notify through their scheme — you receive a compliance certificate without lodging the notice yourself. This is the standard route for domestic homeowners.

## Records for Property Sale and Landlords

Keep a folder (physical or digital) containing:

- EICRs (inspection reports)
- EICs and MEIWCs (work certificates)
- Part P compliance certificates
- Circuit charts photographed after each major job

Buyers’ conveyancers increasingly ask for this evidence on older properties. Landlords face statutory EICR obligations separate from installation certificates for new work — see [EICR for homeowners](/blog/eicr-for-homeowners-when-should-you-get-your-electrics-checked) for the owner-occupier schedule.

## DIY Boundaries Homeowners Often Misread

Replacing a like-for-like ceiling rose on an existing lighting circuit is not the same as adding a new shower circuit. Running armoured cable to a garden room is not the same as changing a broken switch plate.

When in doubt, pay for a registered electrician’s advice before starting — correction costs exceed first-time compliant installation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Part P the same as BS 7671?**  
No. Part P is building regulations; BS 7671 is the technical wiring standard work must meet.

**Do landlords need Part P for every repair?**  
Landlords must maintain safe installations and hold valid EICRs for rentals in England. Notifiable new work still needs certification.

**Can I get retrospective certification?**  
Sometimes via inspection and testing if work is compliant — may require opening up and remedial costs if not.

**Does smart home wiring need Part P?**  
Fixed wiring changes usually yes; battery-powered devices may not involve notifiable fixed work.

**Who enforces Part P?**  
Local authority building control and competent person schemes.
