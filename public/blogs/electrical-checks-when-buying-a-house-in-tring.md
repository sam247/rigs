# Buying a House in Tring? Electrical Checks to Make Before You Complete

You are not legally required to obtain an EICR before buying a home to live in yourself — but checking the electrics before exchange can reveal defects that cost thousands to fix later. At minimum, review any existing electrical certificates, ask direct questions of the seller, and consider an independent EICR if the installation is old or undocumented.

This guide explains what to check during a purchase, how an EICR differs from a mortgage survey, and when to involve a registered electrician before you commit.

## Why Electrics Matter in a Property Purchase

A standard **RICS home survey** (Level 2 or 3) includes a visual check of the consumer unit and some accessories. It does **not** test circuits. Surveyors routinely state that electrical systems have not been inspected and recommend specialist testing where concerns exist.

Electrical defects are not always visible. Problems inside the consumer unit, degraded insulation on hidden cables, and inadequate earthing may only appear on instrument tests. Unaddressed issues can mean:

- Negotiating a price reduction or retention for remedial work
- Delayed completion while safety defects are fixed
- Insurance or mortgage lender questions on older installations

For owner-occupiers there is no legal requirement to hold an EICR at purchase — unlike landlords, who must have a satisfactory report for private rentals in England ([Electrical Safety First — private rented sector](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/what-we-do/campaigning/regulation/private-rented-sector/)). That does not reduce the practical value of checking before you buy.

## Documents to Request From the Seller

Before exchange, ask the seller or their solicitor for:

**Electrical Installation Certificate (EIC)**  
Issued when major work was completed — a new consumer unit, extension circuits, or a rewire. Confirms work was tested to BS 7671 at the time.

**Electrical Installation Condition Report (EICR)**  
A periodic inspection report. Check the date — if it is more than five years old, treat findings as historical. Note any C1, C2, or FI codes.

**Part P / Building Regulations Compliance Certificate**  
Confirms notifiable work was registered. Missing certificates for obvious upgrades (new consumer unit, extension wiring) is a red flag.

**Invoices from registered electricians**  
Helpful where formal certificates were lost but work was recent and traceable.

If none of these exist and the property is decades old with an original-looking fuse board, plan on an independent EICR before exchange if possible.

## What to Look for During Viewings

You cannot certify a installation by sight alone, but these observations warrant follow-up:

- **Consumer unit age and type** — rewirable fuses, plastic enclosures without RCD marking, or overcrowded boards
- **Round-pin sockets or old switches** — suggest wiring predates modern standards
- **Scorch marks, cracks, or buzzing** at sockets and switches
- **Extension leads everywhere** — may indicate insufficient fixed outlets
- **DIY additions** — surface trunking, mismatched plates, outdoor sockets without proper IP rating
- **No visible RCD test button** on the consumer unit

## Should You Pay for an EICR Before Exchange?

An **Electrical Installation Condition Report** is the proper pre-purchase electrical check. A registered electrician inspects and tests fixed wiring and issues coded observations.

**Consider an pre-exchange EICR when:**

- The property is older with no recent certificates
- The survey flagged the electrics for specialist inspection
- You plan major renovation or high-load additions (EV supply, heat pump, electric shower)
- The seller disclosed past electrical problems or insurance claims

**Typical national price guides** quote roughly **£100–£250** for a domestic EICR depending on property size and circuit count — confirm pricing when booking. This is separate from RIGS pricing; contact us for a [Tring EICR quotation](/electrician/tring/eicr-certificates).

An EICR before exchange gives you:

- Documented C1/C2/C3 codes to support negotiation
- A baseline if you proceed — you know what to fix first
- Less surprise after moving in

If the seller already has a recent satisfactory EICR from a registered firm, you may accept it — but check the inspector’s registration and report date.

## How EICR Codes Affect Your Decision

| Code | Meaning | Purchase implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C1 | Danger present | Do not complete without remedial work agreed |
| C2 | Potentially dangerous | Budget for prompt repair; negotiate if extensive |
| C3 | Improvement recommended | Plan upgrades; not always urgent |
| FI | Further investigation | More testing needed before scope is known |

Multiple C2 codes on different circuits often indicate a [full or partial rewire](/blog/do-i-need-a-full-rewire-or-partial-rewire) may be needed. Use our [rewire cost guide](/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-rewire-a-house-in-tring) for **published UK benchmark ranges** — not as a fixed price for the property you are buying.

## EICR vs Homebuyer Survey vs Full Rewire Quote

| Check | What it covers | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RICS survey | Visual only | No circuit testing |
| EICR | Fixed installation test and report | Does not quote remedial work |
| Electrician remedial quote | Cost to fix identified defects | Requires EICR or survey first |
| Rewire quote | Full scope replacement | Requires site survey |

Sequence that works well: **survey → EICR (if advised) → remedial quotes → negotiate → complete purchase → book work**.

## Questions to Ask the Seller and Agent

- When was the property last rewired or partially rewired?
- Has the consumer unit been replaced? Was Part P certification issued?
- Are there certificates for extension or loft conversion electrics?
- Have any circuits tripped repeatedly or been flagged by an insurer?
- Is there an existing EICR? Can we have a copy?

Answers should match what you see at the consumer unit and what certificates show. Inconsistencies warrant an independent inspection.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is an EICR mandatory when buying a house in the UK?**  
Not for owner-occupiers. It is mandatory for landlords letting residential property in England (five-yearly cycle). Buyers often choose one voluntarily.

**Can I renegotiate based on an EICR?**  
Yes — C1/C2 findings with remedial quotes are common grounds for price adjustment, subject to agreement with the seller.

**Will my mortgage lender require an EICR?**  
Most residential mortgages do not require one explicitly, but the survey may recommend further investigation which lenders note.

**How long does a pre-purchase EICR take?**  
Often two to four hours on site for a typical house, plus report issuance. Access to all rooms and the consumer unit is required.

**What if the seller refuses an EICR before exchange?**  
You can book your own after completion, but you lose pre-exchange leverage. Some buyers walk away if electrics look poor and the seller will not disclose certificates.

**Does an EICR cover the electric shower or cooker?**  
It covers the **fixed supply** to those appliances — not the appliances themselves. Portable appliance testing (PAT) is separate.
