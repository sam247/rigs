# How to Tell If Your Fuse Board Needs Replacing

If your home still uses a fuse board with rewirable fuses, lacks RCD protection, or is more than 25 years old, it almost certainly needs replacing. An Electrical Installation Condition Report (EICR) will typically assign a C2 (potentially dangerous) or C3 (improvement recommended) code to such boards because they cannot meet current UK safety standards. Upgrading to a modern consumer unit with RCBOs and a metal enclosure brings your installation in line with BS 7671 and significantly reduces the risk of electric shock and fire.

## Six Warning Signs Your Fuse Board Needs Replacing

You can check many of these signs yourself without opening the board – just look at the front and the surrounding area.

**1. Rewirable fuses**  
If you see a row of ceramic or Bakelite holders with a thin fuse wire, your board is an old rewirable type. These lack RCD protection (30 mA residual current) and cannot accommodate modern surge protection. While not illegal, they will always fail an EICR with a C2 or C3 code.

**2. No RCD or RCBO protection**  
Look for a switch labelled “RCD” or “RCBO”. If there is none, your circuits are unprotected against earth faults. Modern regulations (BS 7671) require RCD protection for all socket outlets up to 32 A and for circuits in bathrooms, showers, and outdoors. Absence of RCDs is a strong indicator that replacement is needed.

**3. Plastic enclosure (pre‑2016)**  
Since January 2016, new or replacement consumer units must be made of or enclosed by non‑combustible material (typically steel). A plastic board can melt or catch fire during a fault. If your board is plastic and was installed before 2016, it does not meet current standards.

**4. No main switch**  
A main switch allows you to isolate the entire board in an emergency. Very old boards may not have one, which is a serious safety deficiency and will likely result in a high‑risk code on an EICR.

**5. Wooden backboard**  
If you can see a wooden board behind the consumer unit, the installation almost certainly dates from before the 1990s. Timber backboards are combustible and no longer acceptable. Replacement of the whole assembly is strongly recommended.

**6. Frequent tripping, overheating, or damage**  
Nuisance tripping, signs of burning or discolouration on the board, brittle wiring, or a smell of hot plastic indicate faults that a new board can correct. Even if the board “works fine”, these symptoms point to deteriorating components.

If you tick two or more of these signs, schedule an inspection by a qualified electrician.

## What an Electrical Safety Inspection (EICR) Will Tell You

An EICR is a formal assessment of the condition of your electrical installation. It codes each defect:

- **C1** (danger present) – immediate action required.
- **C2** (potentially dangerous) – urgent action required.
- **C3** (improvement recommended) – not dangerous now but not compliant.
- **FI** (further investigation required).

An old rewirable fuse board will typically receive a C2 or C3 for lack of RCD protection. Many lenders and insurers now require a satisfactory EICR before a property sale or rental, and a board that fails to meet current standards can hold up the transaction. Even if you are not selling, an EICR every 10 years (or on change of occupancy) is good practice and is recommended by bodies such as NICEIC and the Electrical Safety Council.

## Modern Consumer Units: What to Expect

A modern consumer unit (often still called a fuse box) looks very different from the old boards:

- **Metal enclosure** – non‑combustible, complying with BS 7671 Amendment 2.
- **RCBOs per circuit** – each circuit has its own combined overcurrent and residual current protection, so a fault on one circuit does not knock out power to others.
- **Surge protection device (SPD)** – increasingly required (and strongly recommended) to protect sensitive electronics from voltage spikes.
- **Main switch** – isolates the whole board.
- **Clear labelling** – each circuit is identified, making future work safer.

A replacement is notifiable work under Part P of the Building Regulations. Only a registered competent person (e.g., NICEIC, NAPIT, or ELECSA registered electrician) can self‑certify the installation. They will issue an installation certificate and, if required, an updated EICR. ## How Much Does a Fuse Board Replacement Cost?

Costs vary by property size, number of circuits, and any remedial work discovered during testing. Typical UK prices for a straightforward upgrade (2026) are in the range of **£600 to £1,200**. This usually covers:

- The consumer unit itself (RCBO type, metal enclosure)
- Labour for installation and testing
- Certification and notification to building control

Larger homes with 12+ circuits, or properties needing additional work such as earthing or bonding upgrades, can cost £1,200 to £1,500 or more. If your wiring is old (rubber or lead‑sheathed cable), a full rewire may be necessary, adding £3,000 to £6,000 for a three‑bedroom house. ## Next Steps: Getting a Qualified Electrician

1. **Check registration** – Look for NICEIC, NAPIT, or ELECSA membership. This confirms the electrician can self‑certify work under Part P.
2. **Ask about the inspection** – A good electrician will first carry out an EICR (or a thorough assessment) before quoting. They should explain any faults found and what the replacement will fix.
3. **Get a detailed quote** – It should itemise the unit, RCBOs, labour, testing, certification, and any provisional remedial work.
4. **Ask for certification** – After completion, you should receive an installation certificate and notification to your local building control.

Do not attempt to replace a consumer unit yourself. It is dangerous and non‑notifiable work can invalidate your home insurance and cause legal issues if you sell the property.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I replace my fuse board myself?**  
No. Consumer unit replacement is notifiable work under Part P of the Building Regulations. Only a registered competent electrician can legally carry it out and certify it.

**Will a new consumer unit stop my lights flickering?**  
If the flickering is caused by a loose connection or a faulty circuit, a new board will not fix it – that requires specific fault‑finding. However, if the flickering is due to an RCD nuisance trip or overload on a shared circuit, a modern RCBO board can improve stability.

**Do I need a full rewire with a new consumer unit?**  
Not necessarily. The electrician will assess the existing wiring. If it is in good condition and meets modern standards, they can connect it to the new board. Pre‑1970s rubber or lead‑sheathed cables often need replacement, but this is decided on a circuit‑by‑circuit basis.

**How long does a fuse board replacement take?**  
A straightforward swap usually takes one full day. Remedial work or additional circuits can extend the job to two days.

**Will a new board save me money on electricity?**  
No. A consumer unit does not affect energy consumption. Its main benefits are safety, compliance, and reducing nuisance trips.
