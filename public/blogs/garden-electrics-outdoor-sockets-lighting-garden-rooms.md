# Garden Electrics: Outdoor Sockets, Lighting and Garden Room Power

Garden electrics must be installed by a registered electrician on RCD-protected circuits, using cables and accessories rated for outdoor use (appropriate IP ratings). Running power to a garden room, shed, or outdoor kitchen requires designed circuits — not permanent reliance on extension leads.

Outdoor work is notifiable under Part P in England and Wales when it involves new circuits or work in special locations ([Electrical Safety First — Part P](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/find-a-registered-electrician/part-p-of-the-building-regulations)).

## What Garden Electrics Usually Include

**Outdoor socket outlets**  
IP-rated sockets on external walls or post mounts, on an RCD-protected circuit. Used for lawn equipment, pressure washers, pond pumps, and temporary tools — not as a permanent substitute for designed garden-room supplies.

**Garden lighting**  
Security lights, path lights, uplighting, and feature lighting. Mains-voltage and low-voltage systems have different installation rules. RIGS installs [outdoor lighting](/services/outdoor-lighting) and [security lighting](/services/security-lighting) for domestic properties.

**Garden room, office, or studio supply**  
A dedicated feed from the house consumer unit — often via **SWA (steel wire armoured) cable** underground or protected route — with its own circuit protection. Load calculation covers heating, sockets, and lighting together.

**Shed or garage power**  
Similar fed circuits; consider what tools or equipment will run and whether a sub-board is needed in larger outbuildings.

## Planning Before You Dig or Build

1. **Define use** — storage only, workshop with tools, heated office, outdoor kitchen?
2. **Calculate load** — heaters dominate garden-room load; undersized cable causes voltage drop and nuisance tripping
3. **Route the cable** — underground depth, avoidance of other services, building penetration points
4. **Consumer unit capacity** — spare ways and RCD protection; upgrade may be needed
5. **Building regulations** — garden rooms may need compliance with overall building rules; electrics are one part
6. **Timing** — armoured cable routes are easiest before paving and landscaping finish

Discuss plans with your electrician before the garden room supplier installs internal fit-out — “supply only” trades often expect the client to arrange mains connection separately.

## Underground Cable Principles

Buried cables between house and outbuilding typically use **SWA cable** with appropriate depth and warning tape per BS 7671. Factors affecting design:

- **Distance** — voltage drop over long runs requires larger cable size
- **Load** — amps drawn by heating plus sockets plus lighting
- **Installation method** — direct burial vs ducting
- **Future load** — allowing headroom if use may change

DIY burial of non-armoured cable without proper design is unsafe and non-compliant. A registered electrician tests insulation resistance and earth continuity on completion.

## Outdoor Sockets: Safety Essentials

- **RCD protection** — typically 30 mA for socket outlets
- **IP rating** — IP66 common for exposed outdoor sockets; match product to location
- **Mounting height and position** — away from hose spray paths where possible
- **No indoor sockets** used with weather covers as permanent outdoor solutions
- **Regular checks** — test RCD quarterly; inspect for cracked covers

If an outdoor socket buzzes, smells hot, or trips repeatedly, treat as fault — see [unsafe socket signs](/blog/signs-a-socket-is-unsafe).

## Garden Room vs Extension

A **brick/block extension** wired during build follows [extension electrical planning](/blog/electrics-for-a-house-extension-planning-guide).

A **prefabricated garden room** often arrives without mains connection. The electrician:

- Installs the supply from the house
- Provides consumer unit or feeds a small board in the room if designed
- Certifies notifiable work
- Coordinates with the room supplier’s internal requirements (cable entry point, load spec)

Confirm who supplies what in writing before order.

## Lighting Design Considerations

**Security lighting** — PIR floodlights, dusk-to-dawn sensors, alignment to avoid nuisance triggering of neighbours’ properties. RIGS installs security lighting for driveways and entrances.

**Feature and path lighting** — cable routes, transformer locations for low-voltage systems, and accessible switching.

**Load and switching** — grouping circuits so garden lights do not overload indoor rings.

## Part P and Certification

New garden circuits are notifiable. You should receive:

- Electrical Installation Certificate (or Minor Works where applicable)
- Part P compliance notification
- Diagram of new circuits at the house consumer unit

Uncertificated garden room feeds cause problems at property sale and with insurance.

## Lighting-Only vs Power Circuits

Garden lighting should ideally sit on **dedicated lighting circuits** rather than sharing heavily loaded socket rings indoors. Low-voltage garden lighting still needs mains supply to transformers — locate transformers accessibly and protect cables from garden maintenance damage.

Security lighting overlaps with [security lighting installation](/services/security-lighting) — PIR positioning and neighbour light spill are common design considerations discussed during survey.

## Sheds, Workshops, and Higher Loads

A garden shed used only for storage may need one double socket. A workshop with power tools may require a **higher-rated supply** and RCD protection suitable for portable equipment. Welding, compressors, or kiln loads need explicit load calculation — not ad hoc extension leads.

Tell your electrician the **maximum expected load** and duty cycle when requesting a quote.

## Seasonal and Temporary Use

Festive lighting and temporary pumps should still plug into **RCD-protected outdoor sockets** — not indoor sockets with windows left open for cables. Temporary leads are for short duty; permanent pond pumps and office heating need fixed wiring.

## Maintenance After Installation

- Test RCD devices quarterly
- Keep socket covers closed when not in use
- Trim vegetation away from cable routes and light fittings
- After any garden digging, confirm armoured cable routes with plans before spading

If trips coincide with rain, investigate promptly — moisture ingress worsens with time. Cross-read [unsafe socket signs](/blog/signs-a-socket-is-unsafe) for symptoms.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I run an extension lead to my shed permanently?**  
No — permanent supplies need fixed wiring on appropriate circuits.

**Does a garden room need its own consumer unit?**  
Depends on load and design. Small rooms may be fed from a single circuit; larger setups may need a sub-board.

**How deep should garden cable be buried?**  
BS 7671 specifies minimum depths depending on cable type, protection, and location — your electrician follows the standard for your route.

**Is planning permission required for garden room electrics?**  
Planning permission relates to the structure; electrics must comply with Part P regardless. Check planning rules for the building separately.

**Can garden lights share a circuit with indoor sockets?**  
Sometimes, but dedicated outdoor lighting circuits are often cleaner for maintenance and loading.

**What IP rating for a patio socket?**  
Typically IP66 for exposed outdoor use — confirm product rating matches installation location.
