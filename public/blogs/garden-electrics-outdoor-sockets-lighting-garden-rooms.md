# Garden Electrics: Outdoor Sockets, Lighting and Garden Room Power

**Garden electrics** cover outdoor sockets, garden lighting, and permanent power to sheds, garden rooms, or garden offices — always on RCD-protected circuits with outdoor-rated cable and accessories. Permanent extension leads across lawns are not a substitute for a designed outdoor installation.

Garden electrics must be installed by a registered electrician. Running power outdoors requires designed circuits with appropriate IP-rated accessories. Outdoor work is notifiable under Part P in England and Wales when it involves new circuits or work in special locations ([Electrical Safety First — Part P](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/find-a-registered-electrician/part-p-of-the-building-regulations)).

## What Garden Electrics Usually Include

**Outdoor socket outlets**  
IP-rated sockets on external walls or post mounts, on an RCD-protected circuit. Used for lawn equipment, pressure washers, pond pumps, and temporary tools — not as a permanent substitute for designed garden-room supplies.

**Garden lighting**  
Security lights, path lights, uplighting, and feature lighting. Mains-voltage and low-voltage systems have different installation rules. RIGS installs [outdoor lighting](/services/outdoor-lighting) and [security lighting](/services/security-lighting) for domestic properties.

**Garden room / garden office supply**  
A dedicated feed from the house consumer unit — often via **SWA (steel wire armoured) cable** — with its own circuit protection. Load calculation covers heating, sockets, and lighting together.

**Shed or garage power**  
Similar fed circuits; consider what tools will run and whether a sub-board is needed in larger outbuildings.

## Planning Before You Dig or Build

1. **Define use** — storage only, workshop, heated office, outdoor kitchen?
2. **Calculate load** — heaters dominate garden-room and garden-office load; undersized cable causes voltage drop and nuisance tripping
3. **Route the cable** — underground depth, other services, building penetration points
4. **Consumer unit capacity** — spare ways and RCD protection; upgrade may be needed
5. **Building regulations** — the structure and the electrics are separate compliance paths
6. **Timing** — armoured cable routes are easiest before paving and landscaping finish

Discuss plans with your electrician before the garden room supplier installs internal fit-out — “supply only” trades often expect the client to arrange mains connection separately.

## Garden Room Electrics

**Garden room electrics** usually mean a permanent mains supply from the house to a prefabricated or timber garden room. Typical scope includes:

- A dedicated SWA feed sized for the calculated load
- Circuit protection at the house consumer unit (and a small board in the room where needed)
- Lighting and socket circuits inside the room
- Heating and any high-load appliances called out in the design
- Certification for notifiable work

Agree the **supplier vs electrician split** in writing before delivery. Many packages are shell-only for mains. Confirm cable entry, maximum load, and whether the supplier expects a single feed or multiple circuits. If the room may later become heated living or guest space, design headroom now — upgrading an undersized SWA run after landscaping is far more disruptive.

Brick or block extensions wired during the build follow the [extension electrical planning guide](/blog/electrics-for-a-house-extension-planning-guide) instead — sequencing differs from a freestanding garden room.

## Garden Office Electrics

**Garden office electrics** prioritise reliable sockets for IT, decent desk lighting, and often heating for year-round use. Compared with a storage shed, an office typically needs:

- Enough double sockets for monitors, docks, printers, and chargers without multi-way blocks
- Circuits designed so IT and heating do not nuisance-trip together
- Task lighting plus ambient lighting, often on separate switching
- Heating load included in the cable and protective-device design from day one

Data cabling (ethernet) is separate from BS 7671 power design, but the same trench or wall entry is often used — coordinate with your room supplier or IT installer. If circuits trip when heaters and computers run together, that is a design/load issue, not something to “fix” with extension leads.

## Underground Cable Principles

Buried cables between house and outbuilding typically use **SWA cable** with appropriate depth and warning tape per BS 7671. Design factors:

- **Distance** — voltage drop over long runs needs larger cable
- **Load** — heating plus sockets plus lighting
- **Installation method** — direct burial vs ducting
- **Future load** — headroom if use may change

DIY burial of non-armoured cable without proper design is unsafe and non-compliant. A registered electrician tests insulation resistance and earth continuity on completion.

## Outdoor Sockets: Safety Essentials

- **RCD protection** — typically 30 mA for socket outlets
- **IP rating** — IP66 common for exposed outdoor sockets
- **Mounting** — away from hose spray paths where possible
- **No indoor sockets** with weather covers as permanent outdoor solutions
- **Checks** — test RCD quarterly; inspect for cracked covers

If an outdoor socket buzzes, smells hot, or trips repeatedly, treat as fault — see [unsafe socket signs](/blog/signs-a-socket-is-unsafe). Temporary festive lighting and pumps should still use RCD-protected outdoor sockets, not indoor sockets with cables through windows.

## Part P and Certification

New garden circuits are notifiable. You should receive:

- Electrical Installation Certificate (or Minor Works where applicable)
- Part P compliance notification
- Diagram of new circuits at the house consumer unit

Uncertificated garden room feeds cause problems at sale and with insurance. Wider context: [what electrical work needs Part P certification](/blog/what-electrical-work-needs-part-p-certification).

## Sheds, Workshops, and Higher Loads

A storage shed may need one double socket. A workshop with power tools may need a higher-rated supply and suitable RCD protection. Welding, compressors, or kiln loads need explicit load calculation — not extension leads. Tell your electrician the **maximum expected load** and duty cycle when requesting a quote.

## Maintenance After Installation

- Test RCD devices quarterly
- Keep socket covers closed when not in use
- Trim vegetation away from cable routes and fittings
- Confirm armoured routes before any garden digging

If trips coincide with rain, investigate promptly — moisture ingress worsens with time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I run an extension lead to my shed permanently?**  
No — permanent supplies need fixed wiring on appropriate circuits.

**Does a garden room need its own consumer unit?**  
Depends on load and design. Small rooms may be fed from a single circuit; larger setups may need a sub-board.

**What do garden room electrics usually include?**  
A compliant mains feed from the house, circuit protection, lighting and sockets sized for use, plus certification for notifiable work.

**How are garden office electrics different from a shed supply?**  
Offices usually need more sockets, better lighting, and heating capacity designed in so IT and heaters do not overload a light storage circuit.

**How deep should garden cable be buried?**  
BS 7671 specifies minimum depths by cable type, protection, and location — your electrician follows the standard for your route.

**Is planning permission required for garden room electrics?**  
Planning relates to the structure; electrics must comply with Part P regardless.

**Can garden lights share a circuit with indoor sockets?**  
Sometimes, but dedicated outdoor lighting circuits are often cleaner for maintenance and loading.

**What IP rating for a patio socket?**  
Typically IP66 for exposed outdoor use — match product rating to the location.
