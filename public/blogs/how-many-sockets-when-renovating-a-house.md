# How Many Sockets Do You Need When Renovating a House?

When renovating, plan enough fixed sockets so you are not relying on extension leads — typically at least four to six double sockets in a main living room, four to six in each bedroom, and a dedicated high-density layout in the kitchen. Exact numbers depend on room use, furniture layout, and building regulations for new work.

There is no single legal minimum for every existing room in a refurbishment, but **new build and extension work** in England must meet Part M accessibility guidance and Part P electrical rules. Renovation is the right time to fix decades of under-provision.

## Why Socket Count Matters in a Renovation

Undersocked rooms lead to:

- Extension leads under carpets and furniture — trip hazards and overheating
- Overloaded socket outlets
- Surface trunking added later at greater cost
- Kitchen and home-office layouts constrained by where power happens to be

Renovation opens walls and floors. Adding cable now is far cheaper than returning in five years. Coordinate socket positions with your electrician **before** plasterboard closes.

If the wiring behind existing sockets is obsolete, adding more may require [new circuits or partial rewiring](/blog/can-you-add-sockets-without-rewiring) — not just extra faceplates.

## Room-by-Room Planning Guide

These are **practical planning targets** for modern domestic use, not statutory minimums for every existing property:

**Kitchen**  
The highest density. Plan for worktop sockets at regular intervals (often every 600–900 mm along runs where feasible), plus dedicated circuits for cooker, hob, oven, fridge, freezer, dishwasher, and extraction. See our dedicated [kitchen electrics renovation guide](/blog/planning-kitchen-electrics-during-a-renovation).

**Living room / open-plan area**  
Mark TV, sofa ends, fireplace, desk corner, and charging points. Six to eight double sockets (or equivalent) is common in renovated living spaces — more if open-plan includes dining and workspace.

**Main bedroom**  
Both sides of the bed, dressing area, and optional desk. Four to six doubles is a typical target.

**Secondary bedrooms**  
At least three to four doubles — both sides of bed where layout allows, plus desk wall.

**Home office**  
Dedicated desk wall with four to six doubles, separate from ring load if possible. Consider dedicated circuit if running multiple monitors, printers, and heating.

**Hallway and landing**  
At least one double for vacuum and occasional use; consider USB or lower-power charging points.

**Utility / boot room**  
Washing machine, tumble dryer, freezer — each on appropriately protected circuits.

**Bathroom**  
Sockets only outside zones, RCD protected, IP-rated where required. Shaver outlets in zone 2 where permitted. No standard sockets inside bath/shower zones.

**Garage and garden rooms**  
At least one double internally; external IP-rated sockets on RCD-protected circuits for tools and garden equipment. See [garden electrics](/blog/garden-electrics-outdoor-sockets-lighting-garden-rooms).

## Building Regulations Context

For **new dwellings and certain new rooms**, Approved Document M (England) sets accessibility requirements including socket mounting heights — commonly around 450 mm to 1200 mm from finished floor level for new work in habitable rooms. Your electrician and builder should confirm current requirements for your project type.

Part P applies when you add **new circuits** or alter wiring in special locations ([Electrical Safety First — Part P](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/find-a-registered-electrician/part-p-of-the-building-regulations)).

Renovating an existing room without adding new circuits may allow like-for-like replacement on the same circuit — adding many new points often means new or extended ring/radial design by a registered electrician.

## Height and Position Tips

- **Bedrooms:** doubles at bed head height for lamps and charging
- **Living room:** behind TV (with ventilation gap), sofa arms, and floor-level if using table lamps
- **Kitchen:** above worktop, clear of splash zones for hobs and sinks
- **Avoid** placing sockets where furniture will permanently block them
- **Exterior walls** may affect chase depth in solid-wall properties — discuss routes with your electrician

## Circuits vs Socket Count

More sockets does not always mean more circuits. A ring final circuit supports a defined floor area and socket count under BS 7671 design rules. Dense layouts (kitchen, home office) may need **multiple rings or radials**.

Your electrician calculates load and cable size. If the consumer unit lacks spare ways, a [consumer unit upgrade](/blog/how-much-does-a-consumer-unit-replacement-cost) may be part of the renovation budget.

## Renovation Sequences That Work

1. **Design room layouts** — furniture, kitchen plan, desk locations
2. **Walk-through with electrician** — mark positions on bare walls
3. **First fix** — cables and back boxes before plaster
4. **Second fix** — plates after decoration
5. **Test and certify** — certificates for new circuits

Align with [extension planning](/blog/electrics-for-a-house-extension-planning-guide) if the renovation includes building out.

## Home Office and Living Room Density

With hybrid working commonplace, many renovations dedicate a room or alcove to a desk. Plan **four to six doubles** on the desk wall where monitors, chargers, printer, and desk lighting will sit. Consider whether this load should sit on its own radial rather than the bedroom ring shared with hair dryers and heaters.

Open-plan living-dining spaces benefit from sockets at **both seating areas** and the dining table — avoid a single socket cluster at one end of a long room. Floor sockets exist but require early planning and appropriate IP ratings if near spills.

## USB, Wi-Fi Access Points, and Low-Voltage

USB-A/USB-C socket outlets reduce adapter clutter but still draw load — they count toward circuit design. Data cabling (Ethernet) is not Part P fixed wiring in the same sense as 230 V outlets, but route conduits or ducts during renovation if you want hardwired access points later.

## Accessibility and Future Adaptation

Approved Document M (England) sets expectations for new dwellings and certain new work — including socket mounting heights that reduce the need to bend. If your project falls under these requirements, your electrician and builder should confirm heights before first fix.

Even where not mandatory, slightly higher socket positions in living spaces can help later-life adaptation without another renovation.

## Working With Your Electrician on a Schedule

Provide your electrician with:

- Kitchen plan drawings with appliance specifications (kW ratings for hobs and ovens)
- Bathroom layout with shower type (electric vs pumped vs mains-fed)
- Lighting plan — downlight count and dimming requirements
- Smart-home intentions if [smart switching](/services/smart-home-installation) is part of the project

A single planning meeting before first fix prevents most layout regrets.

## Cost Considerations

Adding sockets during renovation is primarily **labour and cable** on open walls. Published UK guides often cite **£80–£200 per additional double socket** as a very rough benchmark when part of wider work — actual quotes depend on access, circuit design, and finish making-good.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many sockets per room is legal in the UK?**  
New dwellings have regulatory requirements; existing room refurbishments vary. Design to use, not to a single national number for all cases.

**Can I copy my neighbour’s layout?**  
Use their layout as inspiration only — your floor plan and loads differ.

**Are USB sockets worth it?**  
Convenient for charging; many owners mix USB-equipped doubles with standard doubles.

**Do I need an electrician to add sockets in a renovation?**  
New circuits and most additions in special locations are notifiable. Use a registered electrician for compliance and certification.

**Should I future-proof for EV charging?**  
RIGS does not currently offer a dedicated EV charger installation service page — discuss general capacity planning with your electrician if you may add high load later.

**What about smart home switches?**  
Requires neutral at switch positions in many smart dimmer designs — plan at first fix.
