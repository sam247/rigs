# Electrics for a House Extension: What to Plan Before Building Starts

Plan extension electrics before first fix: agree socket and lighting positions with your electrician, confirm the consumer unit has capacity (or budget for an upgrade), and ensure notifiable work will be certified under Part P by a registered installer. Electrical design should run in parallel with architectural drawings — not after walls are closed.

Extensions add new circuits for sockets, lighting, heating controls, and sometimes cooker or shower supplies. Getting the layout wrong is expensive to fix once plasterboard is up.

## Why Electrical Planning Cannot Wait

Builders need to know where to leave:

- Cable routes and draw points for downlights
- Back boxes at the correct heights
- Routes from the extension to the existing consumer unit
- Any structural penetrations for cable entry

If you defer electrics until the extension is weathertight, you may face surface trunking, lost socket positions, or insufficient capacity for kitchen or living areas.

Part P of the Building Regulations applies to new circuits and work in special locations in England and Wales ([Electrical Safety First](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/find-a-registered-electrician/part-p-of-the-building-regulations)). Extension wiring is notifiable. Your electrician should coordinate certification with the wider building project.

## Circuits You Will Likely Need

A single-storey rear extension often requires at least:

- **Socket circuit** — ring or radial depending on load and layout
- **Lighting circuit** — ceiling points, pendants, or downlights
- **Dedicated circuits** — if the extension includes a kitchen area: cooker, hob, oven, microwave, fridge, dishwasher points as designed
- **Heating controls** — thermostats, underfloor heating manifolds, or radiator valves with electrical actuators
- **Smoke detection** — mains-linked alarms where building regulations require them in the extended property
- **Data/TV** — if structured cabling is part of your spec (low voltage, but plan routes early)

Two-storey extensions multiply socket and lighting counts. Loft conversions add similar planning for bedrooms and en-suites — bathrooms need zone-compliant accessories.

## Consumer Unit Capacity

Every new circuit needs a spare way (or a larger board). Before work starts, your electrician should assess:

- Spare ways on the existing consumer unit
- Whether the main switch and tails are rated for additional load
- Whether **RCD/RCBO protection** can be extended correctly
- Main earthing and bonding capacity

If the existing board is already full, obsolete, or lacks RCD protection, a **consumer unit upgrade** may be part of the extension project. Budget using [published UK benchmark ranges](/blog/how-much-does-a-consumer-unit-replacement-cost) — then obtain a property-specific quote.

High-load plans (induction hob, heat pump, EV supply elsewhere on the property) increase supply requirements. Discuss total load with your electrician early.

## Socket and Switch Positions

Walk the empty shell with your electrician and mark:

- Sofa and TV positions
- Kitchen worktop runs — above worktop height per usual practice
- Desk and home-office corners
- Doorways for two-way switching
- Exterior doors for outside light switches

Building regulations in England include accessibility guidance on switch heights in new dwellings — your electrician and builder should align on mounting heights for new build elements. For socket counts by room type, see [how many sockets when renovating](/blog/how-many-sockets-when-renovating-a-house).

## Feeding the Extension From the Main House

Cable routes from the existing consumer unit to the extension must be protected, correctly sized, and installed in permitted zones. Buried cables in walls have depth and routing rules under BS 7671.

Where the extension is detached (garden room, garage), an **underground SWA cable** or protected overhead route may be needed — similar principles to [garden electrics](/blog/garden-electrics-outdoor-sockets-lighting-garden-rooms).

## Testing and Certification

On completion you should receive:

- **Electrical Installation Certificate** covering new circuits
- **Part P notification** via your registered electrician
- Updated circuit chart at the consumer unit
- Alignment with any **EICR** on the original installation if the whole house was tested

Keep certificates with your building completion documents.

## Building Regulations Beyond Part P

Extension projects touch **Approved Documents** for structure, fire, ventilation, and energy — not only electrics. Your builder or architect coordinates overall compliance; your electrician handles the fixed wiring element and certification.

Notify your electrician early if the extension includes:

- **Mains-linked smoke and heat detection** where building control requires it in the extended dwelling
- **Mechanical ventilation** with electrical controls
- **Underfloor heating** manifolds and thermostats
- **Structured cabling** for data, doorbells, or alarm pre-wiring

Early coordination avoids chasing walls twice.

## Staging Work With an Occupied Home

If you remain in the property during extension work, agree power downtime windows with your electrician and builder. Temporary supplies for essential circuits may be possible while new circuits are commissioned.

First fix in the extension often proceeds while the existing house remains live — segregation and safe isolation are the electrician's responsibility. Do not assume extension wiring can be energised before inspection and testing are complete.

## Common Mistakes Homeowners Make

- **Fixing kitchen supplier layouts without electrical input** — appliance positions drive circuit design
- **Under-specifying socket count** — see [socket planning for renovations](/blog/how-many-sockets-when-renovating-a-house)
- **Assuming the builder's quote includes certification** — confirm whether electrical first and second fix are in scope
- **Omitting outdoor or garden feeds** planned for a later phase — ducting is cheaper before paving

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Who designs extension electrics — architect or electrician?**  
Architects set room layouts; a registered electrician designs circuits to BS 7671. Both should collaborate before first fix.

**Is extension wiring notifiable under Part P?**  
New circuits and work in special locations are notifiable in England and Wales. Your registered electrician self-certifies.

**Can I use wireless sockets instead of fixed points?**  
Not as a substitute for fixed socket provision in habitable rooms. Building regulations set minimum outlet requirements in new work.

**How many circuits for a kitchen extension?**  
Often multiple — ring for sockets, dedicated cooker/hob, appliances, lighting, and possibly extraction. Design depends on appliance spec.

**Do I need building control for electrics if I have Part P?**  
Registered electricians notify competent person schemes; building control still oversees the wider extension.

**Should electrics be planned before planning permission?**  
Conceptual load planning helps, but detailed design follows approved layouts.
