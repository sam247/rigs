# Do I Need a Full Rewire or Just a Partial Rewire?

You need a **full rewire** when an EICR or inspection shows widespread deterioration, obsolete wiring types, or multiple C1/C2 defects across the installation — replacing one area would leave unsafe cable elsewhere. A **partial rewire** can be appropriate when testing confirms the rest of the system is sound and only specific circuits, rooms, or additions need new cabling.

The decision is technical, not cosmetic. This guide explains how electricians assess which route is safe, what each option involves, and how cost differs — so you can have an informed conversation before booking work.

## How Electricians Decide: Full vs Partial

The starting point is almost always an **Electrical Installation Condition Report (EICR)** or an equivalent thorough inspection. The electrician is looking for:

- **Cable insulation condition** — rubber, lead-sheathed, or early PVC that has degraded
- **Circuit protection** — whether the consumer unit provides adequate RCD/RCBO cover
- **Earthing and bonding** — main earth and supplementary bonds to gas and water
- **Evidence of unsafe alterations** — non-compliant DIY additions, overloaded circuits, damaged accessories
- **EICR coding** — C1 (danger present), C2 (potentially dangerous), C3 (improvement recommended), FI (further investigation)

If defects cluster on **one circuit or one area** and the rest of the installation tests satisfactorily, partial replacement may be viable. If multiple circuits fail, insulation resistance is poor throughout, or the consumer unit and wiring are the same age and condition, a **full rewire** is usually the safer long-term choice.

Electrical Safety First recommends owner-occupied homes have an EICR at least every ten years. If you cannot remember the last inspection, book one before deciding on rewire scope.

## What a Full Rewire Includes

A full rewire replaces **all** fixed wiring from the consumer unit outward:

- New cables to every socket, switch, and light point on the agreed layout
- A new or fully upgraded consumer unit matched to the circuit design
- Updated earthing and bonding where required
- Testing, certification, and Part P notification by a registered installer

Everything is brought to the same standard at the same time. Future maintenance is simpler because the installation age is consistent and circuits are clearly labelled.

Full rewires are disruptive and more expensive upfront, but they avoid the situation where new wiring in one part of the house is connected to failing cable in another.

## What a Partial Rewire Includes

Partial rewiring targets a defined scope, for example:

- One floor of a house where the other floor was rewired more recently
- A **kitchen or bathroom** refurbishment where circuits in wet zones must meet current standards
- An **extension** wired when the original house was not rewired at the same time
- Specific circuits that failed EICR testing while others passed

Partial work still requires proper design, testing of the **entire** installation on completion, and certification for the work carried out. The consumer unit may need upgrading or additional ways even if only some circuits are replaced — old units often cannot accommodate modern protection devices for new circuits.

A partial rewire is not a shortcut when the underlying installation is failing. It is a targeted remedy when evidence supports it.

## When a Full Rewire Is Usually Necessary

Consider a full rewire if any of the following apply:

- **EICR with multiple C2 codes** on different circuits
- **No RCD protection** and widespread old wiring — upgrading the board alone does not renew degraded cable
- **Obsolete wiring types** (rubber, lead, or unearthable systems) found in more than isolated runs
- **Repeated unexplained tripping** or overheating across several circuits
- **Major renovation** where walls and floors are already open — the marginal cost of full replacement can be lower than doing partial work now and a full rewire later
- **Preparing to sell** — buyers and surveyors increasingly expect a coherent, certificated installation

## When a Partial Rewire Can Be Enough

Partial work may be appropriate when:

- An EICR identifies **isolated C2/C3 issues** on one or two circuits and the remainder is satisfactory
- You are **extending** the property and need new circuits while existing tested wiring remains compliant
- A **recent rewire** covered most of the house but one area was deferred and is now being completed
- **Kitchen or bathroom refit** requires new dedicated circuits in those rooms only, with the rest recently inspected

The electrician should document why partial scope is safe and what limitations remain (for example, older cable types still present in untouched areas).

## Cost Comparison: Full vs Partial

**Full rewire** costs depend on property size; published UK guides for a three-bedroom house often cite **£4,450–£8,000** as a national benchmark range (see our [rewire cost guide](/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-rewire-a-house-in-tring) for sourced figures and disclaimers).

**Partial rewire** costs less because less cable, labour, and accessories are involved — but it is not proportional room-by-room. Consumer unit work, testing the whole installation, certification, and access issues can make a partial job a significant fraction of a full rewire.

| Factor | Full rewire | Partial rewire |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | Entire fixed installation | Defined rooms/circuits |
| Disruption | High — whole property | Localised but may still need power off |
| Future maintenance | Single installation age | Mixed ages — document what remains |
| Typical when | Widespread defects | Isolated defects with sound remainder |
| Quote basis | Full survey | Survey + clear scope boundary |

Always compare **written quotations** for the recommended scope, not a percentage rule of thumb from online guides.

## Partial Rewire Risks to Understand

**Hidden deterioration** — Cable in untouched areas may fail soon after partial work, leaving you to pay mobilisation costs twice.

**Consumer unit limits** — Old boards may lack space or compatibility for modern RCBOs on new circuits.

**Insurance and sale** — A partial certificate documents partial work. Buyers may still ask about the age of untouched wiring.

**Regulatory compliance** — Work in kitchens, bathrooms, and outdoors is notifiable under Part P. All notifiable work must be designed and certified correctly whether full or partial ([Electrical Safety First — Part P](https://www.electricalsafetyfirst.org.uk/find-a-registered-electrician/part-p-of-the-building-regulations)).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can an electrician tell if I need a full rewire without lifting floorboards?**  
A good inspection combines visual checks, sampling, and instrument tests. Sometimes exploratory access is needed if results are inconclusive (FI codes on an EICR).

**Is partial rewiring a bad idea?**  
Not when testing proves the remainder is sound. It is a poor idea when used to avoid replacing clearly failing cable elsewhere.

**Does partial rewiring need Part P certification?**  
Yes, when the work is notifiable — new circuits, consumer unit changes, and work in special locations. Your installer must issue the correct certificate.

**Will a partial rewire fix tripping RCDs?**  
Only if the fault is on the replaced circuits. Nuisance tripping from elsewhere needs [fault finding](/services/electrical-fault-finding) before scope is decided.

**Should I rewire before or after plastering?**  
First fix electrical work (cables) comes before plastering; second fix (sockets, switches, plates) comes after. Coordinate with your builder or renovator.

**How does buying a house affect the decision?**  
If survey or EICR findings are poor, factor rewire scope into your offer. See [electrical checks when buying in Tring](/blog/electrical-checks-when-buying-a-house-in-tring).
